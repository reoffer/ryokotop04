    The $84 mistake that started this test

    It happened in a hotel in Lisbon. I had a deadline, a dead laptop, and a Wi-Fi connection that took 40 seconds to load a single email. The front desk's solution was to charge me $28 a night for "premium" Wi-Fi. I paid it. It didn't help.

    If you've ever paid $15–$30 a day for hotel internet, burned through your phone plan abroad, or missed a call because the airport network collapsed, you already know why this matters. A bad connection isn't just annoying when you're working — it's expensive.

    So we bought 11 hotspots and took them on real trips

    Over six weeks, we tested 11 of the best-selling portable hotspots across 20+ countries — from Lisbon and Tokyo to a rural cabin with one bar of signal. We paid for the devices ourselves, used each one as our primary connection for at least a week, and tracked real download speeds, battery drain, video-call latency, and the total cost of staying online.

    The results were not close. Devices with global SIM technology held steady across borders. Carrier-locked hotspots looked fine in their home market and fell apart the moment we crossed a border. And the cheapest devices often cost the most once data plans and roaming fees were added up.

    Below are the five we'd actually recommend — including the one we didn't expect to win, the one we'd buy if you only travel a few times a year, and the ones that only make sense for specific trips. If you're in a hurry: Ryoko Pro was the only device that stayed connected, stayed fast, and didn't add a roaming bill.

    How We Tested (And What Most Reviews Leave Out)

    We did not test these in a lab or run one speed test on a good day. We took them on real trips and used them the way you would — for work calls, maps, streaming, and the occasional 2 a.m. email. Over six weeks and 20+ countries, we compared them on four things that actually change your experience:

    Real-World SpeedWe ran speed tests in hotel rooms, airports, cafés, trains, and a rural cabin — at different times of day and on different networks. Advertised peak speeds were mostly fiction.
    Coverage That HoldsWe logged every dead zone, manual network switch, and drop to 3G. The best devices switched carriers quietly. The worst made us do it by hand.

    How to Choose a Hotspot Without Getting Burned

    Five questions that matter more than the spec sheet

    Ignore the "global coverage" badge until you read the fine print. Ask how many countries are covered without roaming fees, and whether the plan includes the places you actually travel. Ryoko Pro and Lite cover 175+ countries with no roaming charges; most carrier-locked devices start charging the moment you cross a border.

    Decide whether you want a subscription or a device you own. If you travel constantly, a flat daily rate like Ryoko Pro's $0.49/day unlimited plan is usually cheaper than paying per GB. If you travel a few times a year, Ryoko Lite's pay-as-you-go model means you are not paying for data you do not use.

    Check the battery with a real workload, not a spec sheet. If you are connecting a laptop, phone, and tablet, you need 8+ hours of mixed use, not "up to 12 hours" of standby. In our test, the gap between claimed and real battery life was often 30–40%.

    Count your devices before you buy. Two people with phones and a laptop means at least 5 connections. Ryoko Pro and Lite handle 10; some cheaper hotspots slow down at 3–5. If you are traveling with family or a team, this matters more than top speed.

    Look at the total cost, not the sticker price. Add the device, the plan, per-country fees, and shipping. A $99 hotspot with a $30/month plan and $10/day roaming can cost more than a $0.49/day unlimited plan within a single trip.
